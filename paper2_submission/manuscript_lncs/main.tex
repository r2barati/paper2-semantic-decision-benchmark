\begin{abstract}
Retrieval is normally evaluated by relevance and downstream generation quality.
Many operational deployments instead consume retrieved text through a decision
policy, where the cost of an error depends on which action it induces and on how
the system evolves afterwards. We present an executable benchmark that measures
the value of retrieved evidence by the sequential utility of the decisions it
produces, holding the interpreter and controller fixed. On an operational report
feed with entity-scoped distractors, stale reports and routine traffic, we
compare BM25, TF--IDF and dense retrieval against random and oracle evidence
selection under matched evidence budgets, and pair each system with a fixed
probabilistic interpreter and a receding-horizon inventory controller. Retrieval quality and decision utility do not induce the same ordering. With one
interpreter, every practical retrieval system is worse than retrieving nothing
while the oracle evidence selector is clearly better, and improving nDCG can
lower realised reward; with another interpreter the two orderings agree exactly.
The mechanism is that non-relevant documents are not equally harmful --- a
current report about a different site moves the controller the wrong way,
whereas an off-topic memo only wastes an evidence slot --- so utility is a
property of the pipeline rather than of the ranking. We also show that text-free
controls are a demanding baseline: a constant belief tuned only on development
seeds matches or exceeds most text interpreters, and in one transfer a
shuffled-text ablation is no worse than the genuine interpreter, so positive
effects against an uninformed prior do not by themselves establish the value of
reading text. The benchmark, its frozen artifacts and its controls are released
to support consequence-aware evaluation of information access.
\keywords{Evaluation \and Evidence selection \and Downstream utility \and
Retrieval-augmented decision making \and Calibration.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An operator monitoring a supply network receives a stream of reports. Most
concern other sites; some are stale; a few describe conditions that should change
what they order this period. Choosing which reports to act on is an information
access problem, and its consequences are sequential: an order placed now arrives
several periods later, into a state that earlier decisions produced.

Standard retrieval evaluation scores such a system by topical relevance, and
downstream-aware evaluation such as eRAG scores it by the quality of the text a
model subsequently generates~\cite{salemi2024erag}. Neither captures what the
operator is actually exposed to. Two rankings with identical nDCG can carry
different operational risk, because the ways a document can be non-relevant are
not interchangeable: a current report about a \emph{different} site, written in
the same operational register but describing a different regime, will move a
controller in the wrong direction; an off-topic notice merely consumes an
evidence slot.

We ask a single question: \emph{when does retrieved text improve sequential
decisions?} To answer it we build an executable path from corpus to consequence,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:path}
q,\,\mathcal{D} \;\longrightarrow\; R_k(q,\mathcal{D}) \;\longrightarrow\;
b(z\mid \cdot) \;\longrightarrow\; \pi(a_t\mid s_t,b) \;\longrightarrow\;
s_{t+1} \;\longrightarrow\; U,
\end{equation}

in which a retrieval system $R_k$ selects $k$ documents from a candidate pool
$\mathcal{D}$ under a fixed query $q$, a fixed interpreter maps the selected text
to a belief $b$ over operational regimes, a fixed controller maps state and
belief to an action, and a simulator returns realised utility $U$. Because every
stage after retrieval is held constant, differences in $U$ are attributable to
evidence selection.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(i) A consequence-aware evaluation of evidence selection: retrieval is scored by
the sequential decision utility it induces, reported alongside conventional
retrieval metrics on the same episodes (Section~\ref{sec:retrieval}).
(ii) Evidence that the two orderings diverge, with a mechanism: non-relevant
documents differ in how they mislead, so equal nDCG does not imply equal utility.
(iii) Demanding text-free controls --- constant-belief and development-tuned
no-text operating points, shuffled-text and label-only ablations --- strong
enough to overturn naive readings of positive effects against an uninformed
prior (Section~\ref{sec:controls}).
(iv) A reproducible artifact: frozen corpora, embeddings, checkpoints and
semantic outputs, reconstructible offline without credentials, with the
estimand, weighting and uncertainty declared.

\paragraph{Scope.} The environments and report text are synthetic. We do not
introduce a retrieval model, and we do not claim that any interpreter or
controller is universally preferable. The claim is about \emph{evaluation}: an
information-access system that consumes text through a decision policy should be
scored on the decisions, and the controls that make such a claim credible are
stronger than those typically reported.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Downstream-aware retrieval evaluation.}
eRAG evaluates retrieval by the downstream performance of the language model that
consumes it, showing that upstream relevance can be a poor proxy for end-task
utility~\cite{salemi2024erag}. CUE-R estimates per-evidence utility by
intervention and includes no-retrieval controls, and GroGU makes grounding
utility explicit as a consumer-specific quantity; both are recent preprints and
we treat them as such~\cite{cuer2026,grogu2026}. Our downstream consumer is a
control policy in a stochastic environment rather than a generator, so the
estimated quantity is a trajectory's realised return, not text quality, and the
error asymmetry comes from the environment's cost structure rather than a judge.
That relevance is situational and task-dependent long predates this
work~\cite{borlund2003concept}; our contribution is an executable instrument for
one consequential task, not the observation that consequences matter.

\paragraph{Decision-focused prediction and calibration.}
Predict-then-optimize and decision-focused learning train or evaluate predictions
through a downstream objective~\cite{donti2017task,elmachtoub2022smart,mandi2022rank};
value-aware model learning makes the analogous point for learned
dynamics~\cite{farahmand2017value,lambert2020objective}, decision calibration
formalises the dependence on a decision-maker~\cite{zhao2021decision}, and
value-of-information theory gives the classical account of when information
changes a decision~\cite{howard1966information}. Probability quality is
standardly assessed with proper scores and calibration
diagnostics~\cite{guo2017calibration,degroot1983comparison,niculescu2005predicting}.
We do not learn through the objective: we hold the pipeline fixed and vary the
information supplied to it, which is what makes this an evaluation rather than a
training method. We also separate calibration from the ensembling that
accompanies it in common implementations (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}).

\paragraph{Environment and baselines.}
The multi-echelon simulator derives from an inventory benchmark lineage in
operations research~\cite{hubbs2020orgym}. The retrieval baselines are Okapi
BM25~\cite{robertson1994okapi} and cosine similarity over sentence
embeddings~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert}, scored with
nDCG~\cite{jarvelin2002cumulated}.

\section{Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Objects and estimand}

Let $z$ be a latent operational regime, $\mathcal{D}$ a candidate pool of reports
and $q$ a fixed query expressing a standing information need. A retrieval system
returns an ordered list; the top $k$ documents, $R_k$, are the evidence. A fixed
interpreter maps their concatenation to a belief $b(z)$, and a fixed controller
maps state and belief to an action.

Write $G_i(m)$ for the \emph{realised} return of episode $i$ under information
source $m$,
\begin{equation}
G_i(m) \;=\; \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} r\!\left(s^{(i)}_t, a^{(i)}_t, z^{(i)}\right),
\end{equation}
undiscounted: the horizon is finite and fixed at $T=40$, and any discounting
applied inside the environment is part of $r$. We keep realised returns distinct
from their expectation, which we estimate by the weighted benchmark return
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:jw}
J_w(m) \;=\; \sum_{r} w_r \,
\operatorname*{mean}_{f \in \mathcal{F}_r} \;
\operatorname*{mean}_{v \in \mathcal{V}_{r,f}} \;
\operatorname*{mean}_{i \in \mathcal{S}} \; G_i(m; r, f, v),
\end{equation}
averaging seeds within a template variant $v$, variants within an ambiguity
family $f$, families within a regime $r$, and combining regimes with declared
weights $w_r$. The \emph{balanced} estimand uses uniform $w_r$ and is primary;
a deployment-prior estimand using declared regime frequencies is reported as
secondary. Group sizes are unequal, so \eqref{eq:jw} is not an equal-row mean over
episodes, and the distinction is numerically material.

For a no-information reference $n$ and an oracle-belief reference $o$, the oracle
information value is $\mathrm{OIV} = J_w(o) - J_w(n)$, and the normalised
diagnostic is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sivr}
\mathrm{SIVR}(m) \;=\; \frac{J_w(m) - J_w(n)}{J_w(o) - J_w(n)},
\qquad\text{same } w_r \text{ in numerator and denominator.}
\end{equation}
SIVR is secondary throughout: it is undefined when the denominator is
numerically negligible, flagged when $\mathrm{OIV}<0$, and not bounded above by
one, because $o$ is a perfect \emph{belief} passed through a fixed controller
rather than an optimal policy. Raw paired effects and their intervals come first.
The implementation's $10^{-9}$ guard detects a numerically zero denominator only;
whether $\mathrm{OIV}$ is statistically distinguishable from zero is a separate
question, answered by the interval.

\subsection{Uncertainty}
\label{sec:uncertainty}

The design crosses worlds (seeds) with language (templates): every template
within a regime is evaluated on the same seeds, so seeds are not independent
draws nested under templates. We resample the two axes independently --- one
\emph{shared} seed sample per regime, reused by every family and variant, plus
families and variants with replacement. Intervals are percentile intervals from
$B$ replicates; $p$-values are centred bootstrap test inversions,
$(1+\#\{|b^\ast-\hat\theta|\ge|\hat\theta|\})/(B+1)$, bounded below by $1/(B+1)$
and never reported as exactly zero. With three designed ambiguity families per
regime the family axis is small, so intervals are conditional on the fixed
template set and generalisation to new language remains open.

\section{Experiment R: retrieval quality versus decision utility}
\label{sec:retrieval}

\subsection{Setup}

Each episode is a (seed, regime) pair. The candidate pool contains four kinds of
document, all in the same operational register:

\begin{description}
  \item[relevant] a current report about the operator's own node;
  \item[wrong entity] a current report about a \emph{different} node, drawn
    across all regimes, so some of these errors are actively misleading and some
    are accidentally correct;
  \item[stale] the operator's node, but an event already marked resolved;
  \item[off-topic] routine corporate traffic attributed to the operator's node,
    so it cannot be filtered on entity alone.
\end{description}

Relevance is determined by the query --- entity and recency --- and never by
regime; identifying the regime is the interpreter's job. This separation is what
makes the retrieval task solvable and the dissociation measurable.

Systems: random (floor), BM25, TF--IDF cosine, dense sentence-embedding cosine,
and an oracle evidence selector (ceiling). A no-retrieval control that reads
nothing supplies the paired reference. Evidence budgets $k\in\{1,3,5\}$ are
matched across systems. Interpreters are the rule-based extractor and the
calibrated TF--IDF classifier; the controller is the receding-horizon optimiser.

\subsection{Findings}

\paragraph{The retrieval task is solvable, and the systems separate on it.}
Ranking quality orders the systems random $<$ BM25 $<$ TF--IDF cosine $<$ dense
$<$ oracle, with a wide spread in nDCG@3 (Table~\ref{tab:retrieval}). Belief
accuracy rises monotonically with nDCG for both interpreters, so the upstream
half of the pipeline behaves as intended: better evidence does produce better
beliefs.

\paragraph{Better beliefs do not imply better decisions.}
The downstream half does not follow. With the calibrated TF--IDF interpreter at
$k=3$, \emph{every} practical retrieval system is worse than retrieving nothing:
BM25 loses $74.3$ reward against the no-retrieval control (95\% CI $[-99,-48]$) despite tripling random's nDCG, TF--IDF cosine loses $61.1$, and
only dense retrieval reaches parity ($-1.5$, $[-31,+29]$). The oracle
selector, by contrast, gains $69.0$ ($[+40,+96]$). Ranking quality and
decision utility therefore agree only weakly: Kendall's $\tau_b$ between the two
orderings over the four ranking systems is $+0.33$ at $k=3$ and $0.00$ at $k=1$
and $k=5$ (Table~\ref{tab:rankagreement}). An evaluation that stopped at nDCG
would report steady progress across a region in which realised utility is flat
or negative.

\paragraph{The gap has a mechanism, and it is not noise.}
Non-relevant documents are not interchangeable. The right half of Table~\ref{tab:retrieval} counts what each system
actually puts in front of the interpreter: retrieved documents that are
non-relevant \emph{and} describe a regime other than the true one. Random retrieval surfaces $1.33$ such documents per episode
and BM25 $1.28$ --- BM25 raises nDCG by preferring operationally-worded reports,
but many of those reports are current reports about a \emph{different} node,
which is precisely the error that moves the controller. Dense retrieval improves
because it reduces misleading evidence to $0.87$, not merely because it ranks
relevant documents higher. Standard graded relevance treats an off-topic notice
and a confident report about another site as equally non-relevant; the
controller does not.

\paragraph{Whether the dissociation appears depends on the consumer.}
With the rule-based interpreter the two orderings agree ($\tau_b=1.0$ at $k=3$
and $k=5$) and every retrieval system beats the no-retrieval control. The same
retrieval systems, the same corpus and the same controller yield opposite
practical conclusions under two different interpreters, because the rule-based
extractor degrades gracefully on mixed evidence while the calibrated classifier
becomes confidently wrong. Utility is a property of the pipeline, not of the
ranking alone, which is the concern GroGU raises for grounding
utility~\cite{grogu2026} and which we can here measure end to end.

\paragraph{More evidence is not better.}
The last two columns of Table~\ref{tab:rankagreement} follow the strongest
ranker across budgets. Moving from $k=3$ to $k=5$ raises dense retrieval's nDCG
($0.617 \to 0.691$) while lowering its realised effect under the calibrated
interpreter ($-1.5 \to -18.9$). The additional documents are mostly
non-relevant, and the interpreter has no mechanism for discounting them.

\section{Text-free and degraded-text controls}
\label{sec:controls}

Experiment~R measures evidence selection against a no-retrieval reference. A
second question is what the \emph{text} contributes at all, once a competitive
text-free policy is allowed. We evaluate two transfer environments --- a held-out
multi-echelon demand-surge setting (Experiment~M) and a structurally different
supply-side capacity drop (Experiment~S) --- against constant-belief endpoints, a
no-text operating point tuned only on development seeds, a label-only ablation,
and a shuffled-text ablation in which the interpreter reads a real warning
belonging to a different template.

\input{tables/controls_main}

\paragraph{A constant belief is a demanding baseline.}
In Experiment~M the development-tuned no-text control selects
$p_{\mathrm{event}}=1$ and returns $560.4$, beating single TF--IDF ($516.2$),
calibrated TF--IDF ($530.2$) and GPT-4o ($534.9$), and exceeding the
oracle-belief reference itself ($552.0$). Only the rule-based interpreter
($568.7$) is above it. In Experiment~S the same control returns $1725.1$ against
$1447.0$ for the best interpreter and $1446.3$ for the oracle-belief reference
--- more than three times the oracle information value, without reading anything. Every interpreter's effect against the uninformed
prior is positive in both experiments; in Experiment~S only RuleBased's interval
excludes zero, and none of them establishes value over competitive text-free
operation. The mechanism is visible in the tuning grid:
reward increases monotonically in $p_{\mathrm{event}}$, so the base-stock
controller is systematically under-positioned under the benchmark prior, and any
belief that raises the order-up-to level captures part of that gap without
reading anything.

\paragraph{The shuffled-text control separates the two experiments.}
In Experiment~M, shuffling text destroys the effect: the calibrated interpreter
falls from $530.2$ to $451.1$, below the uninformed prior. Reading the
\emph{right} text is doing real work there. In Experiment~S it does not: the
shuffled control returns $1415.2$, \emph{above} the genuine interpreter's
$1372.9$, even though its belief accuracy is roughly half. Whatever gain the
calibrated interpreter shows in Experiment~S comes from the shape of its belief
distribution moving the controller toward higher stock, not from identifying the
regime. We report this as a negative result: the capacity-drop transfer does not
support a claim that reading the warning is what produced the improvement. Its
wide interval ($[-64,+258]$) also shows how little the frozen template set
constrains that arm.

\paragraph{Label-only beliefs.}
Collapsing the calibrated interpreter to its argmax costs little in
Experiment~M ($530.2 \to 523.5$) and helps in Experiment~S
($1372.9 \to 1392.0$). Probability quality, as measured by proper scores, is
therefore not the operative variable in these transfers, matching what the
controlled benchmark shows (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}).

\section{Calibration is not an isolated intervention}
\label{sec:calibration}

A recurring claim in this literature is that recalibrating a classifier improves
downstream value even when it costs accuracy. The controlled benchmark
(Experiment~C) lets us test that claim carefully, and the care turns out to
matter.

\input{tables/calibration}

\paragraph{The usual comparison is confounded.}
\texttt{CalibratedClassifierCV} does not apply a probability map to a fixed
model: with $k$-fold cross-validation it fits $k$ classifiers and averages them.
A raw-versus-calibrated contrast therefore mixes a calibration map with an
ensembling change. Adding the missing arm --- the same fold ensemble with no
calibration map --- separates them. Ensembling alone \emph{lowers} realised
value (SIVR $0.245 \to 0.154$), and the calibration map then recovers more than
it lost ($0.154 \to 0.338$). The conclusion that calibration helps survives, but
the naive $0.245 \to 0.338$ difference is not attributable to calibration, and
reporting it as such overstates what was shown.

\paragraph{Small calibration sets.}
With three folds over eighteen training texts each calibration set holds six
points. Isotonic regression saturates there, driving some in-sample
probabilities to exactly one; Platt scaling does not. We therefore report both,
and note that the sigmoid arm has higher accuracy ($0.861$ versus $0.806$) but
lower realised value ($0.261$ versus $0.338$). Accuracy costs are also larger
than previously reported once the fold vocabulary is fitted inside each fold
rather than on the full training set beforehand: three held-out texts of
thirty-six, not one.

\paragraph{Probability quality is not the operative variable here.}
The strongest single result in this experiment is negative for the standard
story. Collapsing the raw classifier to its hard label --- discarding all
probabilistic information --- raises SIVR from $0.245$ to $0.773$, more than
either calibrated arm achieves. The label-only ablation has the same accuracy as
the model it is derived from, a better Brier score ($0.222$ versus $0.552$) and a
much worse log-loss ($2.558$ versus $0.932$); the two proper scores disagree
about it, and neither predicts its operational advantage. What the controller
responds to is where the belief mass sits relative to its own asymmetric costs,
which no upstream score measures.

\paragraph{And the no-text control is above all of them.}
The development-tuned constant belief reaches SIVR $0.737$, above every TF--IDF
arm and just below the rule-based extractor ($0.779$). The shuffled-text control
is slightly negative ($-0.062$), so reading the correct text is doing real work
in this environment --- but not enough work to beat a text-free policy that was
allowed a single tuned parameter.

\section{Transfer and boundaries}
\label{sec:boundaries}

Experiment~B perturbs one environment factor at a time around the supply-side
setting. We report \emph{every} variant, including the ones that go the wrong
way, and separate two reasons a normalised value can be negative.

\input{tables/boundary}

\paragraph{Half the variants have no well-defined information value.}
In three of the six variants --- long lead, lost sales and short lead --- the
oracle-belief reference itself underperforms the uninformed prior. The oracle
information value is then negative, the normalised ratio's denominator flips
sign, and no statement of the form ``this interpreter recovers $x$ of the
available information value'' is meaningful. What those rows show is a fixed
controller mismatched to a perturbed environment, not a failure of semantic
sensing, and we mark them rather than reporting a ratio. This is a limitation of
the diagnostic and of the fixed-controller design, and it is the reason raw
paired effects are primary throughout this paper.

\paragraph{Interpreters can still lose badly.}
Under the short-lead variant of Table~\ref{tab:boundary} the calibrated TF--IDF
interpreter returns $2248$ and GPT-4o $2213$ against the uninformed prior's
$2398$, so both lose well over a hundred reward. Halving the lead time leaves the controller with little time to act on a
warning, so a belief that shifts stock upward mostly buys holding cost. An
earlier version of this work omitted the short-lead variant and misreported the
sign of the lost-sales case; both are now in the table, with every other variant.

\paragraph{Text--world pairing.}
The boundary study iterates over sixteen report templates, six of which describe
normal operation. Simulating all of them in a disrupted world turns those six
into false-negative reports. That is a legitimate experiment, but it is an
evidence intervention, not mechanical robustness, and it breaks the text--world
relationship the transfer experiment establishes. The default pairing now
simulates each report in the world it describes; the false-negative arm remains
available and is reported separately when used. Repairing the pairing changes
conclusions: under the previous design the short-lead variant appeared to have a
positive oracle information value, and it does not.

\paragraph{The text-free control again.}
In five of six variants the development-tuned no-text control returns more than
every interpreter, and in the sixth the ordering is unchanged from the baseline.
The controls are not a robustness afterthought; they are the comparison that
determines what the interpreters have shown.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The report feed and the environments are synthetic, single-SKU and
finite-regime. Experiment~R's pools are generated rather than authored, so it
carries no language-axis variance; its query is fixed and its relevance is
defined by construction rather than by assessors. The dense retriever is one
general-purpose sentence encoder with frozen embeddings, not a trained dense
retrieval system, so we make no claim about the ceiling of dense retrieval here.
The controller is fixed and not jointly optimised with the interpreter, so
results describe value under a declared belief-to-action mapping rather than
under every controller. Cached LLM outputs cover a small set of models with
reconstructed rather than certified provenance. Evidence is consumed by
concatenation; belief-level pooling over separately interpreted documents is a
natural variant we do not evaluate.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Information access systems that feed decision policies should be evaluated on the
decisions. In this benchmark, retrieval quality and sequential decision utility
do not induce the same ordering, and the gap has a concrete mechanism: the ways a
document can be non-relevant differ in how far they move a controller from a good
action. The controls matter as much as the systems: a constant belief tuned only
on development seeds is a demanding baseline, and effects measured against an
uninformed prior alone can survive it or not. We release the benchmark, its
frozen artifacts and its controls so that consequence-aware claims about
evidence selection can be checked rather than asserted.

\section{Design, accounting and reproduction}
\label{app:design}

\input{tables/appendix}

\paragraph{Environment, costs and timing.}
Single-item lost-sales inventory over a finite horizon of $T=40$ periods: revenue
$10$ per unit sold, ordering cost $5$ fixed plus $2$ per unit, holding cost $1$
per unit of ending inventory, stockout penalty $8$ per unit of unmet demand,
initial on-hand $30$, Poisson demand with base mean $8$. Three regimes:
\textsc{normal}; \textsc{supplier delay}, raising the lead time during the event
window; and \textsc{demand surge}, multiplying the demand rate. The supply-side
transfer instead reduces a factory's capacity in a multi-echelon network. Within
a period the order is: receive arrivals, realise demand, fulfil from on-hand,
place an order. An order placed in period $t$ under lead time $L$ arrives at the
start of period $t+L$; orders already in transit keep their arrival period, so a
lead-time change applies only to orders placed from that period onward. The
simulator and every planner share one implementation of this schedule, so a
planner's arrival model cannot drift from the dynamics it plans against.

\paragraph{Information release.}
Warning text is published at period $12$; the event window begins at period $20$.
Before publication no interpreter has read anything and every controller plans
under the benchmark prior. From publication the interpreted belief replaces the
prior while on-hand stock and the in-transit pipeline carry over unchanged.

\paragraph{Belief to action.}
The controlled experiments solve a receding-horizon linear program each period
and execute only its first order, with belief-weighted expected lead time and
demand per future period. The multi-echelon experiments use a belief-adaptive
base-stock policy with effective rate $\mathbb{E}_b[\mu]$ and order-up-to target
$\mathbb{E}_b[\mu]\bar{L} + 1.65\sqrt{\bar{L}\,\mathbb{E}_b[\mu]}$. The controller
is identical across information sources within an experiment.

\paragraph{Interpreters, information and retrieval.}
No non-oracle interpreter receives the regime, future demand, future state or
environment internals; the oracle-belief reference receives the true regime and
is a reference, not a deployable system. The TF--IDF classifiers are fitted only
on training templates and evaluated on disjoint held-out ones. LLM beliefs are
replayed from frozen canonical responses, with model labels reconstructed by
re-deriving each cache key from every known (model, text) pair and unmatched
entries labelled \texttt{unknown}. BM25 uses the Okapi form with $k_1=1.5$,
$b=0.75$ and idf floored at zero; dense retrieval is cosine similarity over
frozen \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} embeddings, shipped so the published numbers
reproduce without model weights. Ties break by document id.

\paragraph{Reproduction and impact.}
The release reconstructs its own offline inputs: cached semantic responses come
from a tracked, checksum-verified manifest, and classifier checkpoints are
retrained from tracked templates with fixed seeds; neither step needs
credentials or network access. A verification entry point replays stored beliefs
through the simulator and regenerates every table here from the frozen episodes.
This paper shows that a decision-support component can look better on upstream
metrics while being worse to act on, so deployment needs human oversight and
conservative fallbacks; a synthetic study does not establish deployment safety.
